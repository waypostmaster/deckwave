# Adversarial Audit: Browser-Based Music Analysis and Mixing Engine

> **Researched August 2026.** Third-party versions, terms, prices, quotas
> and browser-support claims below are as of that date and are NOT
> maintained. Re-check anything you intend to rely on.


## TL;DR
- The beat-detection algorithm is a faithful reimplementation of Joe Sullivan's 2014 Beatport article (author correctly identified; "Joe Rocket" is wrong), but it is a naive peak-picking/interval-histogram method whose accuracy tops out around 78% on the author's own hand-picked EDM sample and degrades badly on sidechained, beatless-intro, half/double-time and swung material; modern onset-envelope + autocorrelation/comb-filter or dynamic-programming methods (Ellis 2007, Scheirer 1998, Böck) are portable to plain JS and materially better.
- The composite "energy" index (0.45 RMS + 0.25 ZCR + 0.30 tempo) uses invented weights and a mischosen feature: ZCR is a noisiness/percussiveness measure, not brightness; the literature's arousal predictors use loudness, spectral centroid/flux/rolloff, MFCCs, onset rate and dynamic range, and RMS should be replaced by a perceptual loudness measure (LUFS/EBU R128).
- The ±8% time-stretch cap conflates two things (turntable convention and pitch/tempo JND) and is defensible only as UX policy, not perceptual inaudibility; harmonic mixing is entirely absent (a real gap, cheaply fixable with Krumhansl-Schmuckler key detection at ~75-85% accuracy); and several browser implementation choices (whole-library decodeAudioData, playbackRate for stretch) are memory- and quality-suboptimal.

## Key Findings

### 1. Beat & tempo detection

**Provenance is correct.** The article is "Beat Detection Using JavaScript and the Web Audio API" by **Joe Sullivan**, published at Beatport Engineering (tech.beatport.com) in **2014** (dated November 26, 2014 by third-party mirrors), now hosted canonically at `http://joesul.li/van/beat-detection-using-web-audio/`. The attribution to "Joe Rocket" is simply wrong. The widely-used npm successor is `web-audio-beat-detector` by Chris Guttandin, explicitly "based on the technique explained by Joe Sullivan"; José M. Pérez built a more comprehensive Spotify-preview demo on the same code. Note: the audio author Joe Sullivan is unrelated to the cybersecurity executive of the same name.

**The implementation deviates from the source in one notable way: the folding range.** Sullivan's original folds candidate tempi into **90-180 BPM** (`while (theoreticalTempo < 90) *= 2; while (> 180) /= 2`), and explicitly notes "most dance music is between 120-140BPM." The audited implementation uses **70-180**, a wider, non-canonical band. This matters (see octave errors below). The refractory step in the original is a fixed `i += 10000` samples (~0.227s at 44.1kHz), consistent with the audited "0.25s refractory window." The original counts intervals over the next **10** peaks (`for i<10`), matching the audited description. The descending-threshold search (0.95 down in 0.05 steps until ≥30 peaks) is the standard adaptation used in the npm port.

**Measured accuracy of this specific approach is modest.** Sullivan's own out-of-sample test (22 Jump Street soundtrack, 18 contemporary dance tracks) got **14/18 = 78%** correct, and one track produced "No tempo guesses!" This is a best case: curated, beat-heavy, mostly 4/4 EDM in the 100-160 BPM range. There is no peer-reviewed evaluation of this exact algorithm on standard datasets; it was never benchmarked on GTZAN, Ballroom, etc.

**Known failure modes (well-supported):**
- **Ambient/beatless intros:** Sullivan himself observed "much of the song, especially the beginning and the end, is not being picked up in the peaks" because those sections are quieter. A fixed amplitude threshold on a normalized buffer fails on tracks with long quiet intros — exactly the Mixxx-user complaint about Trance/Psychill/Goa tracks with 1-minute-plus intros throwing off the analyzer.
- **Heavy sidechain compression:** sidechaining pumps the whole mix in time with the kick, which actually *adds* periodic amplitude modulation at the beat rate — sometimes helpful, but it also smears the kick transient and can make the lowpass-peak method lock onto the pumping envelope rather than the true kick, degrading the clean transient the peak-picker relies on.
- **Half-time/double-time:** the interval histogram picks a single modal interval and folds by powers of two, so genuine half-time sections (e.g. dubstep) collapse to one number; the method cannot represent a time-varying tempo and will pick whichever octave dominates the 30-second window.
- **Swing/shuffle:** Sullivan explicitly found tracks with triplet/shuffle drum sections (wasted, midnight-city, express-yourself) produced two top candidates in a 4:3 ratio, and in express-yourself the wrong (higher) one won. Swung rhythms produce bimodal interval histograms that the modal-count method mishandles.
- **Confidence metric is weak:** "modal count / total counts" is not calibrated; it conflates peak density with correctness.

**Better in-browser alternatives (all portable to plain JS, no server/build):**
- **Onset detection functions** — spectral flux (sum of positive bin-wise magnitude differences), complex-domain (magnitude+phase, Duxbury 2003), and high-frequency content are all simple STFT post-processing. Spectral flux is "by far the most popular" ODF; its superflux variant (Böck) adds max-filtering to suppress vibrato false positives. These replace the crude "amplitude peak" step with a proper novelty function and are cheap.
- **Autocorrelation of the onset envelope** — the standard tempo-from-ODF step; Ellis computes global tempo by "autocorrelating the onset function, applying a bias window, and choosing the biggest peak."
- **Comb-filter/resonator tempo estimation** — Scheirer 1998 ("Tempo and Beat Analysis of Acoustic Musical Signals," JASA 103(1):588-601) is the canonical resonator-bank method; more expensive but implementable.
- **Dynamic-programming beat tracking** — Ellis 2007 ("Beat Tracking by Dynamic Programming," J. New Music Research 36(1):51-60, DOI 10.1080/09298210701653344) enforces both onset-match and locally-constant inter-beat-interval via DP; it is compact, widely reimplemented (including a public JS port in audiojs/beat-detection), and gives beat *phase*, not just BPM — essential for beatmatching.
- **BeatRoot (Dixon), madmom (Böck, RNN/DBN), beat-this** — the accuracy leaders but Python/heavier; madmom's RNN+comb approach leads on accuracy but is not trivially portable. The realistic browser target is: spectral-flux ODF → autocorrelation/comb tempo → Ellis DP for phase. Essentia.js (WASM) already exposes these (BeatTrackerMultiFeature, OnsetDetection) in-browser.

**Evaluation metrics and real numbers (specific):** The field uses **Accuracy1** (estimate within ±4% of ground truth) and **Accuracy2** (also accepts ½, ⅓, 2×, 3× the tempo — i.e. forgives octave errors). Key datasets: GTZAN, Ballroom, Hainsworth, SMC, ACM Mirum, ISMIR04, and GiantSteps (EDM). State-of-the-art numbers, from Schreiber & Müller (ISMIR 2018, "A Single-Step Approach to Musical Tempo Estimation Using a Convolutional Neural Network," the "new"/tempo-CNN system) and Böck, Krebs & Widmer (ISMIR 2015, "Accurate Tempo Estimation Based on Recurrent Neural Networks and Resonating Comb Filters"):
- Best **combined Accuracy1 ~74.2%**, combined **Accuracy2 ~92-94%**.
- Ballroom: CNN Acc1 **92.0%**, Acc2 **98.4%**; böck Acc1 **84.0%**, Acc2 **98.7%**.
- GiantSteps (EDM): CNN Acc1 **73.0%**, Acc2 **89.3%**; böck Acc1 **58.9%**, Acc2 **86.4%**.
- SMC (deliberately hard): even the best systems reach only Acc1 **~45%**, Acc2 **~67%**.
- The classic simple method (Percival & Tzanetakis 2014, "Streamlined Tempo Estimation Based on Autocorrelation and Cross-correlation With Pulses," IEEE/ACM TASLP 22(12)) reaches **~98% Accuracy2 on Ballroom/ACM Mirum** but only **~51% Accuracy1 on GiantSteps EDM** (Acc2 ~88%) — a ~37-point Acc1/Acc2 gap that is *entirely octave error*.
- The **Acc1-vs-Acc2 gap is the octave-error signature**: for older algorithms it is 24-33 points; modern deep systems shrink it (CNN Ballroom gap only 6.4 points). The TISMIR 2020 survey (Schreiber, Urbano & Müller, "Music Tempo Estimation: Are We Done Yet?", TISMIR 3(1):111-125, DOI 10.5334/tismir.43) reports modern systems cut the average absolute octave error to as low as **0.056 tempo-octaves**, and explicitly warns against comparing accuracy figures across papers because ground-truth annotations exist in multiple versions (e.g. re-annotating GiantSteps moved böck from Acc1 58.9%→64.8% and Acc2 86.4%→94.0%).

**Is 70-180 defensible?** Partly, and it is the implementation's weakest folding choice. A single-octave fold is standard practice for reducing octave errors, but the *placement* matters. A 70-180 window mis-folds common electronic genres: **drum & bass / jungle (typically 160-180)** sits at the top edge and can fold down; **half-time trap/dubstep** and **psytrance (140-150)** are fine, but **footwork/juke (~160)** is near the edge and **hardcore/gabber (180-200+)** falls outside. The GiantSteps/Beatport EDM tempo work uses **~88-175** or genre-specific ranges precisely because octave errors are range-dependent. Standard practice for octave handling is either (a) a genre/BPM prior, (b) a resonator bank that scores multiple octaves, or (c) a post-hoc octave classifier — one study reported that fixing octave errors alone (an octave classifier on top of existing algorithms) improved performance by 45% and 65.5% on ISMIR04 and MIREX06 respectively. A naive fixed fold with a modal vote, as implemented, is the least robust option.

### 2. Audio features for energy/arousal

**ZCR is the wrong feature for brightness.** Zero-crossing rate counts sign changes per frame and measures **noisiness/percussiveness**, not brightness. librosa's own documentation: ZCR "indicat[es] noisiness or percussiveness... higher in percussive sounds, fricative consonants, or noisy signals." The correct brightness feature is the **spectral centroid** — the magnitude-weighted mean frequency, "the center of mass of the spectrum, correlating with the perceived brightness of a sound." ZCR and centroid are correlated for simple/monophonic signals (both rise with high-frequency energy) but diverge badly on polyphonic music: a distorted guitar has high ZCR; a bright but clean synth pad has high centroid but modest ZCR. Using ZCR as a brightness/energy proxy is a defensible cheap hack for time-domain-only code, but it is technically wrong and should be stated plainly.

**The 0.45/0.25/0.30 weights are invented and not grounded in any published model.** The MIR literature models perceived energy via the **arousal** axis of Russell's valence-arousal circumplex. Published regression models use feature *sets*, not three hand-picked weights:
- The tandfonline "Audio features dedicated to the detection and tracking of arousal and valence in musical compositions" (2018) reports arousal is predicted well (**R² up to ~0.79** with SMOreg SVM after feature selection) using combinations of low-level, rhythm and tonal features from Essentia/Marsyas.
- Weninger et al. reported **R² ≈ 0.65 for arousal** using a 6,373-feature set — only marginally above the **0.64** of a much smaller 44-feature baseline (Aljanaki et al.); valence is much harder (**R² 0.42 vs 0.36**). Arousal is comparatively easy to predict and dominated by loudness/dynamics/timbre.
- Consistent finding across studies: **arousal is driven primarily by loudness/energy, spectral/timbral features (centroid, flux, rolloff, MFCCs), onset rate, and tempo** — so the implementation's three input *families* (loudness, a brightness-ish feature, tempo) are right, but ZCR should be spectral centroid/flux and the weights should be fit, not guessed.

**Spotify's "energy" is a useful reference for what belongs in the index.** Spotify's documented definition (Web API docs, get-audio-features): "Energy is a measure from 0.0 to 1.0 and represents a perceptual measure of intensity and activity. Typically, energetic tracks feel fast, loud, and noisy... Perceptual features contributing to this attribute include **dynamic range, perceived loudness, timbre, onset rate, and general entropy**." Empirically, in a Billboard/Spotify analysis, energy correlates with loudness at **R = 0.7** and anti-correlates with acousticness (**R = -0.57**). This is Spotify developer/marketing language, not a published algorithm — treat the exact construction as proprietary. **Essentia** and **librosa** are the correct open reference implementations for every feature named here.

**Yes, RMS should be replaced by a perceptual loudness measure.** RMS is not perceptually weighted. The broadcast standard is **LUFS / EBU R128** (K-weighted loudness), and **ReplayGain** is the consumer-audio equivalent. For an energy/arousal index whose whole point is perceived intensity, a K-weighted loudness (or at least an A-weighted level) is materially more defensible than raw RMS, and also solves the cross-track normalization problem the index needs anyway.

### 3. Beatmatching & time-stretch limits

**Where ±8% comes from — and why the reasoning is muddled.** The ±8% figure is the classic **Technics SL-1200MK2** pitch-fader range — confirmed by product specs ("Pitch Range +/- 8%") and Technics/audio-database documentation: "the continuous pitch control system of the quartz synthesizer allows the pitch to be changed continuously within ±8%." Later/most modern Technics (MK3D, M5G, MK7) and most DJ gear offer switchable **±8/±16%**, and many CDJs/controllers go to **±100%** or wider with keylock. So "classic turntables have ±8%" is true for the MK2 but the "modern equipment" claim is understated — modern gear routinely exceeds it. Critically, the ±8% turntable range is a *mechanical/UX* convention, **not** a perceptual pitch-shift threshold. Conflating the two is the implementation's error.

**Actual perceptual thresholds.** The just-noticeable difference for **tempo** is roughly **4-8%** depending on listener and method: Levitin & Cook (1996, Perception & Psychophysics 58(6)) note drum machines average 3.5% tempo deviation that "most listeners do not notice... and neither do professional drummers," and found 72% of tapped tempo productions fell within 8% of target ("near the perceptual threshold (JND) for tempo"); Drake & Botte (1993) found tempo JNDs of **6-10% for non-musicians and 3-8% for musicians** (lowest near 100 BPM), while some tempo-*change* JNDs are cited as low as 1-3%. So ±8% of playbackRate is right at the upper edge of where tempo change itself starts being clearly noticeable. But because playbackRate changes pitch too, the JND for **pitch** is far finer — ±8% of tempo-via-playbackRate is roughly a **±1.33 semitone** pitch shift, which is clearly audible to most listeners on tonal material. The honest statement is therefore "±8% keeps artifacts within DJ-convention limits," **not** "the pitch shift becomes inaudible."

**Browser time-stretch options (where the implementation is weakest).**
- **playbackRate changes BOTH tempo and pitch** in Web Audio's AudioBufferSourceNode — there is **no native pitch-preserving time-stretch** in the Web Audio graph. This is the crux: adjusting playbackRate to beatmatch *necessarily* detunes the track, which is exactly why the ±8% cap exists.
- **HTMLMediaElement.preservesPitch** (default `true`; `mozPreservesPitch`/`webkitPreservesPitch` legacy) DOES give free, native, pitch-preserving tempo change — but only on `<audio>`/`<video>` elements, not on AudioBufferSourceNode. Per MDN it is **Baseline (newly available) since December 2023**. If the engine drives playback through media elements it can get keylock for free; if it uses buffer sources it cannot.
- **JS/WASM libraries:** **SoundTouchJS** (time-domain WSOLA; compact, low CPU, runs realtime; now ships an AudioWorklet build replacing the deprecated ScriptProcessor). **Rubber Band via WASM** (rubberband-wasm / rubberband-web with ready AudioWorklet) — highest quality (frequency-domain R3 engine), but high CPU: the vendor notes R3 is ~3× slower than R2, and Rubber Band's CPU load was historically too high for realtime mobile. **Tone.js GrainPlayer / PitchShift** (granular; cheap but audible graininess). Trade-off: SoundTouch = cheap/acceptable, Rubber Band = expensive/excellent.

**True beatmatching ≠ tempo matching.** Matching BPM is necessary but not sufficient. Real beatmatching requires: (1) **beat-grid detection** (per-beat positions, not just a global BPM); (2) **downbeat/phase alignment** so beat 1 lines up with beat 1; and (3) **cue-point handling**. Mixxx is the reference open-source implementation. Its analyzer wraps the **Queen Mary (qm-dsp) beat tracker**: complex-domain ODF (DF_COMPLEXSD), 1024-sample frames / 512 hop, Butterworth smoothing, a **resonator comb-filter bank** for tempo, **Viterbi decoding** for the tempo contour, and **dynamic-programming backtracking** for beat positions (the "New" method is explicitly a hybrid of Davies 2007 two-state tracking and **Ellis 2007 DP**). Mixxx then snaps to a constant "hard" BPM (80/96/120/144) for grid alignment. Its **master sync** matches both tempo *and* phase; the manual notes a beatgrid error as small as **0.02 BPM causes audible drift over a long club track**, and describes the "double bass kick" sound when kicks drift out of phase. The audited engine, which only nudges playbackRate to match BPM with no phase/downbeat model, does **tempo matching, not beatmatching** — it will drift and will not align downbeats.

### 4. Harmonic mixing (currently absent)

**The "clashing keys will clash" note is honest, but the gap is cheaply fixable.** Practical in-browser key detection:
- **Krumhansl-Schmuckler** key-finding: build a 12-bin chroma/pitch-class profile from the audio, correlate (Pearson) against 24 stored key profiles (12 major + 12 minor), pick the max. Profile variants exist: original **Krumhansl-Kessler (1982)** probe-tone profiles, plus **Temperley (1999)** and **Albrecht-Shanahan** refinements that reduce errors. This is implementable in plain JS on top of a chroma feature (Meyda/Essentia.js provide chroma) and runs fully client-side.
- **Accuracy:** ~**75-85% MIREX-style** on clearly tonal Western music (pop/rock/classical/jazz); lower on atonal/modal/ambient/heavily-autotuned material. The classic failure mode is **relative-major/minor confusion** (C major vs A minor share notes) and dominant/tonic confusion — so always surface an alternative-key list. MIREX uses a **weighted score** (1.0 exact, 0.5 perfect fifth, 0.3 relative, 0.2 parallel). Best CNN systems (Korzeniowski/Widmer; MIREX 2017 CNN submissions) reach the low-to-mid 80s weighted on some sets (e.g. 82.33 on Billboard, 77.14 on Robbie Williams) but genre-specific tonality remains hard (EDM GiantSteps ~68).

**Camelot wheel & does harmonic mixing measurably help?** The **Camelot wheel** (Mark Davis, ~1990; popularized by Mixed In Key) relabels the circle of fifths as 1-12 + A(minor)/B(major); the rule is "adjacent numbers, or same number switch letter." It is a genuinely useful *practitioner* abstraction. However, the claim that harmonic mixing "measurably improves perceived mix quality" is **practitioner folklore, not established science** — the strong claims ("you'll easily hear the difference," "an unconscious sense of wrongness") come from DJ-education and vendor sources (DJ.Studio, Mixed In Key, Dubspot), not controlled listening studies. There is little peer-reviewed evidence quantifying the perceptual benefit. The defensible position: key detection + Camelot suggestions are a valuable feature many professionals rely on, but the engine should present it as a compatibility heuristic, not a proven quality guarantee.

### 5. Browser implementation correctness

- **Whole-library decodeAudioData is not a scalable memory approach.** `decodeAudioData` decodes the *entire* file to uncompressed float PCM in RAM (a 4-minute stereo track ≈ ~80-85 MB at 44.1kHz/32-bit float). Decoding a whole library at once will exhaust memory. For analysis, decode **one track at a time**, or analyze a 30-second slice (as Sullivan/Pérez do), release the buffer, and for playback prefer streaming via `<audio>` + MediaElementAudioSourceNode. `decodeAudioData` also "only works on complete files, not fragments," so it cannot stream a partial download.
- **OfflineAudioContext for prefiltering is correct and idiomatic.** Rendering the source through a BiquadFilter in an OfflineAudioContext (exactly Sullivan's approach) is the right way to apply the lowpass/highpass prefilter faster than realtime. The lowpass-150Hz/highpass-30Hz kick isolation is reasonable, though a bandpass or a dedicated low-band ODF would be cleaner. One correctness note: a single biquad lowpass at Q=1 is a gentle 12 dB/oct slope — bass-heavy non-kick content (basslines, sub) will leak in.
- **AudioWorklet, not ScriptProcessor, for any realtime analysis.** ScriptProcessorNode is **deprecated** (MDN/W3C) and runs on the **main thread**, causing glitches; **AudioWorklet** runs on the dedicated audio render thread and is the current standard. For *offline* analysis via OfflineAudioContext neither is needed.
- **decodeAudioData browser differences are real.** Supported codecs differ (historically Safari/WebKit required a valid explicit buffer size for ScriptProcessor; codec support for MP3/AAC/Opus/FLAC varies by browser and OS); resampling to the context sample rate and channel handling can differ. Test decode across engines and check `AudioBuffer.sampleRate`.
- **Web Workers can be used for analysis — with a caveat.** `AudioContext`/`OfflineAudioContext` are **not available in Web Workers** (they are window-scope). But you do NOT need an AudioContext to analyze raw PCM: decode on the main thread (or via WebCodecs `AudioDecoder`, which *is* worker-available), then **transfer the Float32Array PCM to a Worker** and run FFT/onset/tempo/key math there off the main thread. This is the correct architecture for a responsive UI and is fully compatible with a no-build-step, no-server design.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — correctness fixes (cheap, do now):**
1. Fix the attribution to **Joe Sullivan** and cite the canonical `joesul.li` URL.
2. Replace **ZCR with spectral centroid** (and optionally spectral flux) for the brightness/energy term; keep ZCR only as a supplementary noisiness feature.
3. Replace **RMS with a K-weighted/LUFS (EBU R128) or at least A-weighted loudness**; this also gives cross-track normalization.
4. Narrow or genre-condition the tempo fold. Change the default from **70-180 to 90-180** (matching the source) or, better, choose the fold from a coarse genre/BPM prior. Document that a single naive fold is octave-error-prone.
5. Stop decoding the whole library at once — decode/analyze per track on a 30s slice and release buffers; move FFT/tempo/key math into a **Web Worker**.

**Stage 2 — capability upgrades (moderate effort):**
6. Replace the amplitude-peak beat detector with a **spectral-flux ODF → autocorrelation/comb tempo → Ellis-2007 DP** pipeline (or adopt Essentia.js). This gives beat **phase**, enabling real beatmatching, and is far more robust to quiet intros and swing.
7. Add **Krumhansl-Schmuckler key detection** (Temperley/Albrecht-Shanahan profiles) on a chroma feature, expose **Camelot codes** and an alternative-key list, and warn on low confidence. Present harmonic compatibility as a heuristic, not a guarantee.
8. For pitch-preserving tempo changes, route playback through **HTMLMediaElement with preservesPitch** (free keylock, Baseline since Dec 2023) or integrate **SoundTouchJS (AudioWorklet)** for buffer-source playback; reserve **Rubber Band WASM** for offline/high-quality rendering given its CPU cost.

**Stage 3 — do it properly (larger effort):**
9. Implement a **beat grid + downbeat/phase alignment + master-sync** model in the style of Mixxx (constant-BPM grid snap, phase sync, cue points). Benchmark against Mixxx output.
10. Fit the energy/arousal weights on a labeled dataset (or adopt an Essentia arousal model) rather than hand-picking 0.45/0.25/0.30.

**Thresholds that would change these recommendations:** If the target library is exclusively steady-4/4 EDM in a narrow BPM band and phase alignment is not required, the Sullivan method + playbackRate cap is *adequate* and Stages 2/3 are optional. If the engine must handle live/acoustic/variable-tempo music, or must produce genuinely beat-locked mixes, Stages 2-3 are mandatory. If CPU/mobile is a hard constraint, prefer SoundTouch over Rubber Band and do key detection once at import rather than in realtime.

## Caveats
- Sullivan's 78% figure is from an 18-track curated EDM sample, not a standard benchmark; treat it as an optimistic ceiling, not general accuracy.
- Tempo/key accuracy numbers cited are from ISMIR/TISMIR primary sources, but the TISMIR 2020 authors explicitly warn that cross-paper comparisons are unreliable due to multiple ground-truth annotation versions; treat all percentages as indicative.
- Spotify's "energy" construction is documented in developer/marketing docs, not a peer-reviewed spec; its exact algorithm is proprietary.
- The perceptual benefit of harmonic mixing is practitioner consensus, not established experimental science; the sources asserting an audible quality difference are DJ-education/vendor pages.
- Pitch/tempo JND figures vary with musical training, stimulus, and method; ~4-8% for tempo and sub-semitone for pitch are representative ranges (Levitin & Cook 1996; Drake & Botte 1993), not universal constants.
- Browser behavior (codec support, preservesPitch, WebCodecs availability) evolves; verify current support matrices at implementation time.
